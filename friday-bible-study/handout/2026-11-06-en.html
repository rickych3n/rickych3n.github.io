Scripture | Matthew 12:15–21 (WEB)

15 Jesus, perceiving that, withdrew from there. Great multitudes followed him; and he healed them all,

16 and commanded them that they should not make him known,

17 that it might be fulfilled which was spoken through Isaiah the prophet, saying,

18 “Behold, my servant whom I have chosen, my beloved in whom my soul is well pleased. I will put my Spirit on him. He will proclaim justice to the nations.

19 He will not strive, nor shout, neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets.

20 He won’t break a bruised reed. He won’t quench a smoking flax, until he leads justice to victory.

21 In his name, the nations will hope.”

Introduction

In an age when “the loudest voice wins,” people are accustomed to seizing power through volume and trampling weakness with force. Contentious argument at work, online campaigns of public condemnation, and even rivalry in families and relationships all celebrate the law of the jungle. Yet when the sovereign King of the universe entered history, His way of exercising rule and winning victory overturned worldly logic. He did not shout or strive, nor was His voice heard in the streets. The sign of His victory was that He stooped to protect a bruised reed and sustained a faint, smoldering flame with overflowing compassion.

Today, let us open Scripture and enter the revelation of Isaiah 42 and Matthew 12. We will see how this suffering Servant-King establishes true justice throughout the earth through grace and obedience unto the cross.

🔗 Chain-Reference Study, Verse by Verse

Verse 15: “Jesus, perceiving that, withdrew from there… and he healed them all”

Context BeadMatthew 12:14“But the Pharisees went out, and conspired against him, how they might destroy him.”

“Perceiving that” means perceiving this very plot. Jesus’ withdrawal is the first action of this passage — and the key to the whole passage.

Core BeadMatthew 8:17“that it might be fulfilled which was spoken through Isaiah the prophet, saying: He took our infirmities and bore our diseases.”

Right after the healings in chapter 8, Matthew had already quoted Isaiah 53:4 to string Jesus’ healing to the “Suffering Servant.” Now in 12:15, facing a plot to kill him, Jesus still “healed them all” — Matthew links healing and the Servant a second time. Withdrawal and healing happen together: what he withdraws from is conflict, never compassion.

Side BeadMatthew 14:13“Now when Jesus heard this, he withdrew from there in a boat to a deserted place apart. When the multitudes heard it, they followed him on foot from the cities.”

When Jesus heard of John the Baptist’s death, he too “withdrew to a deserted place”; in Matthew, withdrawing is always a re-direction, never a flight.

Verse 16: “and commanded them that they should not make him known”

Core BeadMatthew 8:4After cleansing the man with leprosy, Jesus likewise charged him: “See that you tell nobody.”

Charging those who were healed not to spread the word is a recurring pattern in Matthew. It is not a publicity strategy but the very character the prophet foretold — the quotation in the next verse says at once that the Servant “will not shout.” He does not advance his mission by fame, because his mission was never accomplished by noise.

Side BeadMark 1:44–45“See that you say nothing to anybody, but go show yourself to the priest, and offer for your cleansing the things which Moses commanded, for a testimony to them.” But he went out, and began to proclaim it much, and to spread about the matter, so that Jesus could no more openly enter into a city, but was outside in desert places. People came to him from everywhere.

But the man went out and began to proclaim it much, so that Jesus could no longer openly enter a city — the contrast brings out the purpose behind Jesus’ charge.

Verses 17–18a: “Behold, my servant whom I have chosen, my beloved in whom my soul is well pleased. I will put my Spirit on him”

Matthew says all this was to fulfill what was spoken through Isaiah the prophet; the quotation is Isaiah 42:1–4 — the first of the four “Servant Songs” (Isaiah 42, 49, 50, 52:13–53:12). From here to v21, every line of the quotation has beads to string.

Core Bead 1Matthew 3:16–17 (the Baptism)“Behold, the heavens were opened to him. He saw the Spirit of God descending as a dove, and coming on him. Behold, a voice out of the heavens said, ‘This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased.’”

The baptism scene is Isaiah 42:1 acted out live — “I will put my Spirit on him” matches the Spirit descending; “in whom my soul is well pleased” matches the Father’s voice. That voice itself strings two Old Testament texts together: Psalm 2:7, “You are my Son,” plus Isaiah 42:1, “in whom my soul is well pleased.”

Core Bead 2Matthew 17:5 (the Transfiguration)“This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased. Listen to him!”

In Matthew the Father speaks from heaven only twice, and both times it is the same sentence, drawn from Isaiah 42:1. “Listen to him” strings further to Deuteronomy 18:15, the prophet “like Moses.”

Side BeadIsaiah 11:2“Yahweh’s Spirit will rest on him: the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and of the fear of Yahweh.”

Another source for the Messiah’s anointing by the Spirit.

Side BeadIsaiah 49:1–3“Listen, islands, to me. Listen, you peoples, from afar: Yahweh has called me from the womb; from the inside of my mother, he has mentioned my name. He has made my mouth like a sharp sword. He has hidden me in the shadow of his hand. He has made me a polished shaft. He has kept me in his quiver. He said to me, ‘You are my servant, Israel, in whom I will be glorified.’”

The second Servant Song: the servant is “named from the womb,” echoing “my beloved.”

Verse 18b: “He will proclaim justice to the nations”

Core BeadIsaiah 42:6–7“and make you a covenant for the people, as a light for the nations, to open the blind eyes, to bring the prisoners out of the dungeon.”

In the same chapter Isaiah says it even more fully: the Servant’s work is not only to “proclaim justice,” but to bring light to the nations and lead people out of darkness. Matthew 12:15 — “he healed them all” — is this commission beginning to be fulfilled.

Side BeadIsaiah 49:6“He says, ‘It is too light a thing that you should be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob, and to restore the preserved of Israel. I will also give you as a light to the nations, that you may be my salvation to the end of the earth.’”

Verse 19: “He will not strive, nor shout, neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets”

Core BeadIsaiah 53:7“He was oppressed, yet when he was afflicted he didn’t open his mouth. As a lamb that is led to the slaughter, and as a sheep that before its shearers is silent, so he didn’t open his mouth.”

The end of the Servant Songs (the fourth song) pushes “quiet” to its limit — not never speaking, but not answering back when oppressed. The quiet of 12:19 is not mere mild temperament; it is the Isaiah 53 kind of quiet, on its way to the cross.

Core Bead 21 Peter 2:23“who, when he was reviled, didn’t revile back. When he suffered, didn’t threaten, but committed himself to him who judges righteously.”

Peter strings Isaiah 53 straight into the life of the church: as Christ was, so are those who are called. This bead carries the text from “the life of Jesus” to “our example” (1 Peter 2:21).

Side BeadIsaiah 50:6 (the third Servant Song)“I gave my back to those who beat me, and my cheeks to those who plucked off the hair. I didn’t hide my face from shame and spitting.”

Verse 20a: “He won’t break a bruised reed; he won’t quench a smoking flax”

Core Bead 1Ezekiel 34:4, 16“(O shepherds of Israel) …you haven’t bound up that which was broken…” → “I will bind up that which was broken, and will strengthen that which was sick.”

Ezekiel rebukes shepherds who treat the sheep the Pharisees’ way: the injured left untended, the lost never sought. God says: what you will not do, I will do myself. Matthew 12:20 is that promise fulfilled — those the Pharisees would “break” (the hungry disciples, the man with the withered hand, the sick) the Servant binds up, one by one.

Core Bead 2Matthew 11:28–30“for I am gentle and lowly in heart. Take my yoke upon you, and learn from me.”

Three weeks ago we studied “Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened.” Then it was Jesus’ own spoken invitation; by Matthew 12:20 Matthew tells us this gentleness is no mere word of comfort — it is the Servant’s portrait the prophet wrote eight hundred years earlier. The invitation of 10/9, the mercy of 10/23 (Hosea 6:6, “I desire mercy, and not sacrifice” — quoted by Matthew twice, at 9:13 and 12:7), and the not-breaking of 11/6 meet here.

Side BeadIsaiah 40:11“He will feed his flock like a shepherd. He will gather the lambs in his arm, and carry them in his bosom. He will gently lead those who have their young.”

Side BeadIsaiah 57:15“I dwell in the high and holy place, with him also who is of a contrite and humble spirit, to revive the spirit of the humble, and to revive the heart of the contrite.”

Verse 20b: “until he leads justice to victory”

Core BeadIsaiah 42:4“He will not fail nor be discouraged, until he has set justice in the earth.”

Notice a small difference: Isaiah’s original stresses that the Servant “will not fail nor be discouraged, until he has set justice in the earth,” while Matthew’s quotation (close to the Septuagint) puts it as “until he leads justice to victory.” Together they give the whole meaning of the passage — the gentle one does not lose heart, and his end is victory, not emptiness.

Core Bead 2Revelation 5:5–6“Behold, the Lion who is of the tribe of Judah… has overcome… I saw… a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain.”

The last time Scripture speaks of this King’s “victory,” his form is a Lamb that had been slain. The way justice wins never changes, from Isaiah 42 to Revelation 5: not by shouting, but by self-giving.

Verse 21: “In his name, the nations will hope”

Core BeadRomans 15:12Paul quotes Isaiah 11:10 — “There will be the root of Jesse, he who arises to rule over the Gentiles; in him the Gentiles will hope.”

Writing to Rome about Gentile believers, Paul uses a sentence almost word-for-word the same as Matthew 12:21. The seed Matthew planted early in his Gospel, Paul sees flowering in his letter.

Core Bead 2Acts 13:47Paul and Barnabas, declaring at Pisidian Antioch that they were turning to the Gentiles, quote Isaiah 49:6: “I have set you as a light for the Gentiles, that you should bring salvation to the uttermost parts of the earth.”

The apostles take the Servant Song’s words as their own commission — the Servant’s mission is carried on by the church. Luke 2:32, where Simeon holds the infant Jesus and calls him “a light to lighten the Gentiles,” is this thread’s first stop within Jesus’ own life.

Closing BeadMatthew 28:19“Go therefore and make disciples of all nations.”

The final command of Matthew’s whole Gospel is 12:21 brought to completion. The Jewish leaders conspired to destroy him, yet the nations would hope in him — Matthew carries this contrast from chapter 12 all the way to chapter 28.

The Whole Chain in One Sentence: The Pharisees conspired to destroy him (12:14), yet he withdrew, healed, and did not shout — because eight hundred years earlier Isaiah had already written it: God’s beloved Servant (Isaiah 42), anointed by the Spirit (Matthew 3:17), who does not break a bruised reed (Ezekiel 34; Matthew 11:28), who opens not his mouth when oppressed (Isaiah 53:7), who leads justice to victory as a slain Lamb (Revelation 5:6), so that the nations hope in his name (Romans 15:12), until all nations become his disciples (Matthew 28:19).

Study Questions

Question 1

Scripture (Matthew 12:14–15a): “But the Pharisees went out, and conspired against him, how they might destroy him. Jesus, perceiving that, withdrew from there.”

Question: What did Jesus know, and why did He withdraw?

Answer: He knew that the Pharisees had plotted to destroy Him (12:14). Jesus’ withdrawal was not weak surrender or fearful avoidance; it was obedience to God’s timetable in salvation history and a complete rejection of worldly political confrontation. Through the quotation, Matthew shows that the Messiah did not answer the murderous intent of religious authorities with fleshly violence. Instead, by withdrawing and ministering in mercy, He displayed the anointed pattern of the suffering Servant, fulfilling God’s appointed work.

Question 2

Scripture (Matthew 12:15b): “Great multitudes followed him; and he healed them all.”

Question: Who followed Jesus? What did He do for them, and what does “he healed them all” tell us?

Answer: Great multitudes followed Him, and He healed all their sick. “All” means without exception: even as hostility increased, He did not withdraw His compassion.

Question 3

Scripture (Matthew 12:16): “and commanded them that they should not make him known.”

Question: What did Jesus command the people, and what apparent tension does this create with His public healing of so many?

Answer: He told them not to make Him known—not to publicize Him. He did not advance His mission through fame or publicity, preparing us for “He will not strive, nor shout” in 12:19.

Question 4

Scripture (Matthew 12:17): “that it might be fulfilled which was spoken through Isaiah the prophet, saying,”

Question: Why does Matthew say this happened to fulfill what Isaiah had spoken?

Answer: Jesus’ withdrawal and quietness were not a failed plan; they were the very manner of God’s Servant foretold in the Old Testament. Everything unfolded within God’s plan (Isaiah 42:1–4).

Question 5

Scripture (Matthew 12:18a): “Behold, my servant whom I have chosen, my beloved in whom my soul is well pleased. I will put my Spirit on him.”

Question: What does God call this Servant? How does God regard Him, and what does God give Him?

Answer: God calls Him “my servant,” chosen and beloved, in whom His soul is well pleased, and places His Spirit upon Him. This echoes the heavenly voice and the descent of the Holy Spirit at Jesus’ baptism (3:16–17).

Question 6

Scripture (Matthew 12:18b): “He will proclaim justice to the nations.”

Question: What is the Servant’s mission? What is justice, and to whom will it be proclaimed?

Answer: He will proclaim justice to the Gentiles. Here “justice” means the order and truth of God’s righteous, covenantal reign—His redemptive order and purpose revealed to all humanity through the Messiah. It goes beyond a merely forensic judgment of condemnation; it includes the establishment of God’s saving righteousness and the manifestation of His kingdom rule, bringing light and justice to nations once in darkness. From the outset, the mission reaches beyond Israel to all nations.

Question 7

Scripture (Matthew 12:19): “He will not strive, nor shout; neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets.”

Question: What manner of ministry is described by “He will not strive, nor shout, neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets”?

Answer: He does not quarrel, shout to build momentum, or seize the public stage. This sharply contrasts with the Pharisees’ faultfinding, argument, and plotting (12:2, 10, 14).
“He will not strive” means He does not work through quarrels and opposition merely to win.
“Nor shout” means He does not advance His mission through loud publicity or rallying crowds.
“Neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets” means He does not seek public exposure.
Together they describe one posture: He does not prevail by self-promotion or by overpowering others. This does not mean Jesus never spoke or reasoned, nor is it an excuse to avoid conflict or to remain silent when justice should be sought. Many then expected a conquering king who would seize power by force; this Messiah is different.

Question 8

Scripture (Matthew 12:20a): “He won’t break a bruised reed. He won’t quench a smoking flax,”

Question: What kind of people are the “bruised reed” and “smoking flax”? How does the Servant treat them, and how does that differ from the Pharisees?

Answer: They are extremely vulnerable people whom others may regard as almost useless: the man with the withered hand, the hungry disciples, and all the sick. The images point concretely to marginalized people crushed by burdensome legal regulations, low social standing, illness, and poverty (that is, the “people of the land,” am ha'aretz, despised by the rabbinic class). Religious leaders saw them as useless as a broken reed or unpleasant as a smoking wick about to go out. Jesus saw people to be gently cherished and healed.

Question 9

Scripture (Matthew 12:20b): “until he leads justice to victory.”

Question: What does “until he leads justice to victory” teach? Is the Servant’s gentleness weakness or retreat?

Answer: No. Gentleness is the way He brings justice, and the victory of justice is the certain end He will achieve. His way does not abandon justice; through gentleness He brings justice to final victory.

Question 10

Scripture (Matthew 12:21): “In his name, the nations will hope.”

Question: Who, at the end of the quotation, will trust in His name? How does this contrast with those who oppose Him?

Answer: The Gentiles. Jewish leaders sought to destroy Him, but the Gentiles would place their hope in His name. This also anticipates the gospel going to all nations (28:19).

Passage Summary

When Jesus learned that the Pharisees wanted to destroy Him, He quietly withdrew. Many still followed Him, and He healed all their sick while instructing them not to publicize Him. Matthew says this fulfilled Isaiah’s prophecy: God has a Servant whom He has chosen, loved, and delighted in. Filled with God’s Spirit, He will bring God’s justice to the Gentiles. This Servant does not quarrel or create a public commotion. He will neither break those already bruised nor extinguish those nearly spent, until God’s justice fully triumphs. In the end, even the Gentiles will place their hope in His name.

Expository Theme

Scripture (Matthew 12:18–21): “Behold, my servant whom I have chosen, my beloved in whom my soul is well pleased. I will put my Spirit on him. He will proclaim justice to the nations. He will not strive, nor shout, neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets. He won’t break a bruised reed. He won’t quench a smoking flax, until he leads justice to victory. In his name, the nations will hope.”

Question: Through Jesus’ withdrawal and healing, and by quoting Isaiah, what does Matthew tell readers about the kind of Messianic Servant Jesus is and how He brings God’s justice?

Answer: Jesus is God’s chosen, Spirit-anointed Servant. He does not rely on contention or publicity; He brings justice with gentleness that does not break the weak. He will surely lead justice to victory and become the hope of the nations.

Application Principle

Scripture (Matthew 12:19–20): “He will not strive, nor shout, neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets. He won’t break a bruised reed. He won’t quench a smoking flax, until he leads justice to victory.”

Question: Having received Christ’s gentle mercy, how should believers face their own weakness and live in His likeness amid a loud and contentious world?

Answer: Believers may bring their weakness to Christ, who does not break a bruised reed. By the Holy Spirit He gives, they can bear witness to His justice with a gentleness that neither contends nor breaks the weak, because He will surely bring justice to victory.

Leader’s note: The point is not a self-imposed demand that “I must be gentler.” Christ first treated us bruised reeds with gentleness and gave us His Spirit. Our gentleness is a likeness He forms in us, not an achievement we force from ourselves. Nor does gentleness mean having no convictions. Jesus also spoke sternly to hardened opponents (12:34). His gentleness is mercy toward the weak and the repentant, always directed toward the victory of justice.

Application Questions

Question 1

Scripture (Matthew 12:19): “He will not strive, nor shout; neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets.”

What recent conflict or discussion—with family, in a group chat, or on social media—made me especially want to contend? Was my first inward response to imitate Christ’s obedience, or to win by fleshly force and public opinion?

Question 2

Scripture (Matthew 12:20): “He won’t break a bruised reed. He won’t quench a smoking flax, until he leads justice to victory.”

When I repeatedly fall into an old sin, feel dry and powerless in prayer, and think I am useless, how can I hold fast to Jesus’ promise?

Question 3

Scripture (Matthew 12:19–20): “He will not strive, nor shout, neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets. He won’t break a bruised reed. He won’t quench a smoking flax, until he leads justice to victory.”

How should we understand Christ’s gentleness and refusal to strive? Does it mean never stating a position? How should we express and seek justice? When we speak, how can we discern between contentiousness and speaking truth?
